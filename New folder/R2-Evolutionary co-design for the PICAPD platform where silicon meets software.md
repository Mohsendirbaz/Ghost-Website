# Evolutionary co-design for the PICAPD platform: where silicon meets software

**The PICAPD architecture's core design tension — where to draw the boundary between hardware-fixed logic and software-defined physics — has clear answers from industry practice and recent research, though several specification gaps pose significant risk.** The fixed-point sensor ingestion formats (Q1.15, Q16.16) are adequate for classical sensors but destroy quantum sensor precision by up to 13 orders of magnitude. The dynamic safety tolerance (7-bit `tol` field) is certifiable under ISO 26262 ASIL-D with careful engineering, following precedents set by Mobileye RSS and NVIDIA Safety Force Field. The CSR-based operand sourcing for physics injection is architecturally conservative and well-aligned with every major automotive SoC's fixed-datapath-plus-configurable-parameters pattern. And the vibration-adaptive hibernation challenge has a direct solution in neuromorphic adaptive thresholding, which has demonstrated **67% energy reduction** in noisy environments. This report examines each design boundary in detail, grounding PICAPD's novel features in the state of the art.

## The precision cliff between Q-formats and quantum sensors

The PICAPD architecture's most critical unresolved design risk lies at the conversion boundary between fixed-point sensor ingestion and FP64 physics kernels. Industry practice universally defines an explicit conversion point — NXP's S32R45 radar processor uses fixed-point for front-end DSP (beamforming, FFT, CFAR) and floating-point for matrix operations (angle-of-arrival, Kalman filtering). Cadence's Tensilica ConnX radar DSPs follow the same pattern. Intel/Altera's radar FPGA reference designs explicitly describe "data moving directly from the fixed-point front-end beamforming to the floating-point pulse compression and Doppler stages." The PICAPD specification leaves this boundary unspecified, which is the single largest architectural gap.

For classical sensors, the specified Q-formats are broadly adequate at the ingestion stage. **Q1.15 provides ~96 dB SQNR**, sufficient for 12–16 bit radar ADC data. Q16.16 offers ~96 dB dynamic range for position and velocity, covering the precision requirements of conventional MEMS IMUs and GPS receivers. Research on radar FFT processing (Heo et al., *Sensors* 2021) shows that 28-bit fixed-point FFT requires **3× more DSP blocks and 2× more BRAM** than hybrid floating-point on Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+, while Texas Instruments demonstrated that 16-bit block floating-point FFT produces SNR rivaling full floating-point. The optimal intermediate format for radar FFT stages is therefore block floating-point or 24+ bit fixed-point, not the Q1.15 specified for raw I/Q ingestion.

For Quantum 2.0 sensors, however, the fixed-point formats are catastrophically insufficient. Chip-scale atomic clocks achieve timing stability of **1 part in 10¹⁸** — the Microchip SA.45s CSAC delivers 1.5×10⁻¹⁰ Allan deviation at one second, with next-generation optical clocks (Infleqtion Tiqker, deployed on Royal Navy's Excalibur autonomous submarine in October 2025) achieving laboratory-grade precision. Q1.15 format provides only ~5 decimal digits of fractional precision, which is **13 orders of magnitude too coarse** for quantum clock timing. Even FP64, with ~15.9 significant decimal digits, is borderline for 10⁻¹⁸ stability. Quantum magnetometers (Fraunhofer IAF's NV-center diamond sensors achieving picotesla sensitivity) and cold-atom inertial sensors (achieving 10⁻⁷ m/s² acceleration bias stability) face similar precision destruction through Q-format ingestion. The architecture must either bypass fixed-point stages entirely for quantum sensor data or introduce dedicated 64+ bit fixed-point ingestion paths.

The FP64 choice for physics kernels is well-justified. **No published implementation of fixed-point Verlet integration exists for production systems.** The Verlet position update involves subtracting two nearly-equal position values — a catastrophic cancellation scenario where fixed-point arithmetic loses all significant digits. All molecular dynamics and physics simulation codes use FP64 minimum. For Kalman filtering, research by Smidl and Peroutka (*IEEE Trans. Industrial Electronics*, 2012) established that square-root decomposition algorithms (Bierman-Thornton, Carlson-Schmidt) are **mandatory** for fixed-point EKF implementations, as standard covariance-form EKF diverges in limited precision. The PICAPD's M-registers (128×64-bit for moments μ₀–μ₃) correctly target FP64 for these computations. One emerging alternative worth monitoring is the posit number format (Gustafson, 2017): P(32,2) posits can sometimes safely replace FP64 for sensor fusion, offering better accuracy near 1.0 and wider dynamic range per bit than IEEE 754.

## Dynamic safety tolerance is certifiable but demands a fail-strict default

The PICAPD's 7-bit tolerance field — where `tol=0` disables checks and `tol=127` enforces ε ≈ 1.28×10⁻⁸ — maps directly onto an established pattern in automotive safety: context-adaptive safety envelopes. ISO 26262 does not explicitly prohibit dynamic tolerance adjustment, but imposes strict constraints that fundamentally shape how this feature must be implemented.

Mobileye's Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) framework provides the strongest precedent. RSS defines a **parameterized mathematical safety model** with customizable parameters including response time, maximum braking force, and minimum lateral distance — parameters that adjust the safety envelope dynamically based on driving context. Shalev-Shwartz, Shammah, and Shashua's original formulation (2017) explicitly states the boundary between utility and safety is "a dynamic one that can change across geographies and time." NVIDIA's Safety Force Field (SFF) extends this by computing safety envelopes frame-by-frame from forward physics simulation, with built-in margins that account for real-world reaction time and perception uncertainty. Both frameworks are accepted by industry and standards bodies, establishing that context-dependent safety strictness is viable.

The ISO 26262 constraints for ASIL-D hardware are demanding but navigable. The standard requires **≥99% Single Point Fault Metric, ≥90% Latent Fault Metric, and <10⁻⁸ probabilistic dangerous failures per hour**. For dynamic tolerance to comply, four conditions must hold:

- The tolerance-setting mechanism must itself meet ASIL integrity requirements or be decomposed with sufficient independence via ISO 26262-9, Clause 5
- Freedom from Interference must be proven between the QM/lower-ASIL software setting tolerance and the ASIL-D hardware executing conservation checks
- A documented safety concept must demonstrate that at every tolerance level, residual risk meets safety goals
- **The safe default must be strictest** — `tol=127` should be the default/fallback, with relaxation only when conditions are verified

The current specification's `tol=0` (checks disabled) is problematic for ASIL-D. No safety-critical ASIL-D system should permit complete disabling of safety checks without an independent redundant mechanism. The Infineon AURIX family and Cadence Tensilica FlexLock processors — both ASIL-D certified — offer configurable safety parameters (watchdog timeouts, voltage monitor thresholds, memory protection regions), but none permit complete safety bypass. The PICAPD should either remove `tol=0` as an option for safety-critical instructions or gate it behind an ASIL-D-rated authorization mechanism.

ISO 21448 (SOTIF) adds another dimension: the tolerance adjustment mechanism must account for "unknown unsafe" scenarios where check strictness might be insufficient for an unrecognized situation. Fraunhofer IKS research (2023) demonstrated a physics-based safety model where configuration depends on the active Operational Design Domain, deriving thresholds for minimum braking capacity and time-to-safety in accordance with ISO 26262. This provides a certification-compatible methodology for the PICAPD: define a mapping from ODD states to minimum `tol` values, verify this mapping through HARA analysis, and enforce it in hardware.

Trust Score registers (256×32-bit) align with established patterns in Byzantine fault-tolerant multi-agent systems. Research on CP-WBFT (2024) demonstrates trust-modulated consensus that "requires unanimity for extremely sensitive actions, majority for routine ones" — directly analogous to using trust scores to modulate conservation check strictness. The Vector Clock registers for causal ordering are standard distributed systems primitives with well-understood formal properties.

## Vehicle vibration demands adaptive perturbation baselines

The PICAPD's kernel hibernation mechanism — EPUs entering DEEP_SLEEP when perturbation δS falls below threshold — faces a well-characterized adversary in vehicle vibration. Cabin-mounted electronics experience **0.02–0.05 g RMS on highway (ISO 8608 Class A roads), 0.05–0.15 g RMS in urban driving (Class B/C), and 0.15–0.5 g RMS off-road (Class D+)**. The dominant vibration energy concentrates in the 1–25 Hz band from road excitation, with engine harmonics producing 0.5–2.5 g peaks at 38–44 Hz. ISO 16750-3 requires automotive electronics to maintain full operation (Functional Status A) through 8 hours of random vibration per axis at these levels. AEC-Q100 qualification demands zero failures across 77 samples per lot through 20g–70g frequency sweeps from 20 Hz to 2,000 Hz.

The solution draws directly from neuromorphic computing and event camera research. The NeuEdge framework (2025) implements adaptive threshold mechanisms on Intel Loihi 2 that dynamically adjust neuron firing thresholds based on input activity statistics: thresholds increase during low-activity periods (reducing energy by 67%) and decrease during high-activity events (maintaining sensitivity). This achieves **89% hardware utilization** versus 47% for naive mapping, while using **4.7× fewer spikes** through hybrid temporal coding. The Sony/Prophesee dynamic vision sensor paradigm provides an even more direct analogy: each pixel stores a reference brightness and fires only when change exceeds a contrast threshold (typically 10–15% for commercial sensors), with the reference automatically resetting after each event. Delbruck et al. (CVPRW 2021) demonstrated real-time feedback control of event camera thresholds, adjusting contrast sensitivity to regulate event rates — measuring noise event rate, computing signal-versus-noise, and using proportional control to optimize.

For PICAPD's Perturbation Detection Units, a multi-layered approach emerges from the research:

- **Moving baseline estimation**: Continuously estimate the vibration-induced δS floor using an exponential moving average. Set the wake threshold as baseline + k×σ, where σ is the estimated standard deviation of noise-induced δS fluctuations. This is the DVS camera paradigm applied to computation.
- **Frequency-domain rejection**: Vehicle vibration concentrates below 200 Hz. A digital high-pass or band-stop filter on δS rejects known vibration frequencies. MEMS sensor noise rejection demonstrates that narrowing bandwidth from 1000 Hz to 1 Hz reduces noise by **√1000 ≈ 31.6×**.
- **Common-mode rejection across EPUs**: Quantum gravity gradiometers achieve vibration immunity by measuring differences between two atom clouds, suppressing common-mode vibration noise. If multiple EPUs share the same physical substrate, differential perturbation detection between EPU groups — reacting only to perturbations not correlated across all units — distinguishes genuine computational events from mechanical vibration without any mechanical isolation.
- **Co-located MEMS accelerometer compensation**: The Muquans/Exail atomic gravimeter achieves sub-μGal resolution in urban environments purely through active software compensation using a classical accelerometer co-located with the quantum sensor. An AEC-Q100-qualified MEMS accelerometer (e.g., STMicroelectronics AIS25BA: 80 μg/√Hz noise density, ±7.7g range, 266 μs latency) can provide the vibration reference signal.
- **Hysteresis and refractory periods**: Implement hysteresis on wake/sleep transitions and a minimum refractory period after each wake-up to prevent vibration-induced rapid cycling. NeuEdge's refractory-period-like control is a primary contributor to its 67% energy reduction.

The **116 pJ power budget** for the full EPU hierarchy is achievable even with these additions. The μBrain fully event-driven architecture demonstrates **26 pJ per spike** in 40nm CMOS, and Intel Loihi 2 operates at 250–500 mW for 1 million neurons. The key constraint is that vibration-adaptive thresholding adds analog front-end complexity to the Perturbation Detection Units — but this is well within the design envelope of AEC-Q100-qualified automotive ASICs.

## CSR-based physics injection trades pipeline efficiency for equation flexibility

The PICAPD's use of CSR[mvariational] at address 0x806 to supply rs3/rs4 operands for VERLET and HAMILT instructions is a well-characterized pattern in RISC-V architecture with understood tradeoffs. A critical paper from CARRV 2022 (Blaise and Kim, Georgia Tech) directly addresses CSR-based operand passing for custom RISC-V extensions, identifying it as "the least efficient" of three approaches because CSR execution stalls the pipeline — the RISC-V specification enforces **no-reordering during execution of CSR instructions**, adding one extra instruction per invocation and reducing IPC.

However, "least efficient" does not mean "wrong." The key optimization is **loop-invariant CSR hoisting**: when the physics equation doesn't change across loop iterations (same mass matrix, same time step), the CSR write can be hoisted to the loop preamble. This amortizes the overhead to near-zero for tight physics integration loops — exactly the workload VERLET and HAMILT target. Only when the equation changes per iteration (different physics at each step) does the overhead reach its worst case of ~50% IPC reduction for physics instructions. Tesla's FSD chip NPU achieves **80% MAC utilization** with only 5 instructions precisely because neural network inference naturally amortizes configuration costs over many operations per configuration change.

The broader industry pattern strongly validates PICAPD's approach. Every major automotive SoC uses **fixed execution datapaths with software-configurable parameters**:

- **Mobileye EyeQ Ultra** (5nm, 176 TOPS): Four accelerator classes — XNN (fixed-function CNN), PMA (CGRA with near-fixed-function compute density but programmable), VMP (VLIW+SIMD), MPC (multi-threaded) — all using 12 RISC-V cores for orchestration
- **NVIDIA DRIVE Orin**: DLA 2.0 fixed-function accelerator achieves **2.5× power efficiency over GPU** by fixing the datapath while programming weights through TensorRT compilation
- **Tesla FSD Chip**: Custom NPU with 96×96 MAC array and only 5 instructions — maximally fixed-function for their workload, with evolving neural network weights loaded via OTA updates
- **Qualcomm Snapdragon Ride Flex**: Fuses ADAS and cockpit on single SoC with hardware isolation, using configurable DSP and NPU accelerators

The physics-programmable ASIC literature provides direct precedent for register-configurable equation solvers. A memristor-based ODE solver (Nature Communications, 2025) supports arbitrary-order differential equations via three configurable modes, achieving **601×–6,920× speedup** over CPU/GPU through programmable conductances — not silicon changes. Reconfigurable photonic PDE solvers (Nanophotonics, 2024) demonstrate 90–93% accuracy versus commercial solvers with electro-optically reconfigurable node weights. In every case, the solver structure is fixed in hardware while equation parameters are stored in configurable registers — exactly the PICAPD pattern.

The CARRV 2022 paper notes that CSR-based operand passing is most appropriate when the ISA is "subject to changes in the future" and the extra operands change infrequently relative to compute operations. Both conditions hold for PICAPD. Future silicon revisions could widen the instruction format (using R4-format encoding) to encode more operands directly if profiling shows specific physics equations dominate, following the established ASIP (Application-Specific Instruction Set Processor) co-design methodology where the initial flexible design is progressively hardened based on empirical workload data. Stanford's AHA (Agile Hardware) methodology, which automatically generates accelerator hardware and compilers from a single specification, provides a formalized framework for this evolutionary approach.

## Quantum 2.0 sensors are a future upgrade, not a present constraint

The PICAPD's quantum sensor targeting is architecturally forward-looking but temporally premature for most modalities. **Chip-scale atomic clocks are the only TRL 9 quantum sensor** — Microchip's SA.45s CSAC has shipped over 100,000 units since 2011 at ~$1,500–$5,000 per unit, with 17 cm³ volume and 120 mW power. For autonomous vehicles, CSACs enable 3-satellite GPS positioning (versus 4 normally required), critical for urban canyons. Cost must drop below ~$100 for broad automotive adoption, expected in 5–10 years.

Quantum LiDAR in its "quantum-inspired" form — single-photon avalanche diode arrays measuring individual photon time-of-flight — is at TRL 4–5. Heriot-Watt/MIT systems resolve 1mm features at 325m range using superconducting nanowire detectors. A quantum-inspired all-fiber LiDAR (Nature Communications, 2023) achieves **>100 dB noise rejection** with single-photon sensitivity. True entangled-photon LiDAR remains TRL 2–3 and faces fundamental source-power limitations that prevent competing with classical LiDAR on range.

Quantum magnetometers (NV-center diamond sensors) have been validated in deep-sea navigation (National Science Review, 2025) and flight tests, achieving ~50× error reduction over pure INS across ~1000 km trajectories. But positioning accuracy remains on the order of hundreds of meters — useful for coarse navigation aiding, not centimeter-level autonomous driving localization. Quantum inertial sensors (cold-atom interferometers) deliver extraordinary stability but are limited to ~1 Hz bandwidth and 0.1–1 m³ volume, requiring hybridization with classical MEMS IMUs. Automotive-compatible form factors are **8–15 years away**.

The architecture should therefore be designed to accommodate both classical and quantum sensor inputs, with quantum sensors as progressive upgrades. The fixed-point ingestion formats work for classical sensors today; quantum-specific high-precision ingestion paths can be added in future silicon revisions as quantum sensors achieve automotive-grade SWaP (size, weight, and power) specifications.

## Certification requires a deliberate strategy from day one

**No neuromorphic or event-driven processor has achieved ASIL-D certification** as of early 2026. This is the PICAPD architecture's most significant market-entry barrier. The certification gap stems from a fundamental tension: ISO 26262 requires deterministic, documented behavior for safety-critical paths, while event-driven architectures inherently depend on input-dependent timing.

The certification pathway exists but demands deliberate architectural choices. The Safety Element out of Context (SEooC) mechanism in ISO 26262 enables developing chips without complete system knowledge, provided documented Assumptions of Use and Development Interface Agreements are maintained. ASIL decomposition (ISO 26262-9, Clause 5) allows the Worker→Manager→Queen hierarchy to serve as natural redundancy: independent processing paths in Workers enable cross-checking at the Manager level, with the Queen providing final arbitration. Texas Instruments achieved effective ASIL-D for the TMS320 DSP through ASIL-D systematic capability combined with dual-unit ASIL-B probabilistic hardware.

Specific safety mechanisms required include dual-core lockstep (or the TMR.VOTE/BYZ.CONS instructions serving an equivalent function), ECC for all memory operations, transient fault protection for internal registers, diagnostic error injection capability, and comprehensive FMEDA with fault injection testing. Synopsys has established an ASIL-D systematic development flow certified by SGS TÜV Saar, and Cadence's Tensilica Xtensa with FlexLock provides the closest existing precedent — ASIL-D-certified processors that are customizable through the TIE instruction extension language, directly analogous to PICAPD's custom ISA extensions.

The 3.4ns decision latency target is physically achievable for hardwired final-stage decisions. At ~300 MHz clock, 3.4ns equals approximately one cycle. AMD's FPGA PMA demonstrates 2.5ns transmit-plus-receive latency, and QBayLogic achieved 500ns end-to-end for EtherCAT in just 13 clock cycles. The hierarchical EPU architecture naturally supports this: Workers handle preprocessing at microsecond scale, Managers aggregate at sub-microsecond, and the Queen executes final safety-critical decisions through minimal-depth combinational logic paths.

## Conclusion: five design decisions that will define PICAPD's viability

The evolutionary co-design analysis reveals that PICAPD's architectural choices are broadly sound when measured against industry practice, but five specific decisions will determine whether the platform succeeds.

**First, the fixed-point-to-FP64 conversion boundary must be explicitly specified.** Every production automotive signal processing chip defines this boundary. For PICAPD, conversion should occur at the output of front-end DSP (after FFT/CFAR) and before any Kalman filtering or physics computation. Block floating-point should be considered for intermediate radar FFT stages where Q1.15 accumulates quantization noise.

**Second, `tol=0` must be eliminated or heavily gated for safety-critical paths.** The architecture should default to `tol=127` (strictest) with relaxation requiring verified ODD conditions, following the RSS parameterized safety model. The tolerance-setting mechanism itself must meet ASIL integrity requirements.

**Third, Perturbation Detection Units need a specified adaptive thresholding algorithm.** The DVS camera paradigm — reference storage with automatic reset after events, plus proportional feedback control — provides a proven, hardware-efficient approach. A co-located MEMS accelerometer enables vibration compensation at negligible cost.

**Fourth, CSR-based physics injection should be treated as the first iteration, not the final design.** Profile production workloads to determine which physics equations dominate, then harden the most common equation paths with direct register encoding in future silicon while keeping CSR-based injection for rare or novel equations. This follows the standard ASIP co-design lifecycle.

**Fifth, certification planning must begin immediately.** The SEooC pathway with ASIL decomposition across the Worker→Manager→Queen hierarchy is the most viable route to ASIL-D, but requires safety architecture decisions that propagate through every level of the design. The TMR.VOTE and BYZ.CONS instructions provide natural safety mechanisms, but their interaction with dynamic tolerance and trust scores must be formally analyzed for freedom from interference. The 10,000:1 data compression target — achievable through cascaded event-driven sensing (100×), sparse representation (10×), and learned compression (10×) — must be validated to not discard safety-relevant information at any compression stage.
