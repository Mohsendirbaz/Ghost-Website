The American experiment rests on a paradox. From its earliest days, the nation has wrapped itself in the language of divine selection—a "city upon a hill," a beacon of freedom and virtue destined to illuminate the world. This mythology runs so deep that it pervades classroom curricula, political speeches, and popular culture. Children learn it alongside the alphabet. Politicians invoke it reflexively. It has become not merely patriotism, but something closer to civic religion.

Yet beneath this gleaming narrative lies a more complicated truth, one that the national story has systematically obscured. The men and women who first populated the British American colonies were not, for the most part, exemplars of virtue seeking religious freedom or entrepreneurial opportunity. They were Europe's unwanted: convicts transported under court order, debtors fleeing creditors, indentured servants sold into temporary slavery, and religious extremists expelled from their communities for views too radical even for their fractious times.

Consider the raw numbers. Between 1718 and 1775, Britain transported between 50,000 and 120,000 convicted criminals to its American colonies under the Transportation Act. This was not a minor footnote but a central pillar of colonial population policy—Britain solving its prison overcrowding problem by shipping its criminal class across the Atlantic. Virginia, the crown jewel of British America, received an estimated 75% of its immigrants as indentured servants, many of whom arrived not by choice but by deception, kidnapping, or economic desperation so acute that seven years of bondage seemed preferable to starvation.

Georgia, founded in 1732, was explicitly designed as a debtor colony. James Oglethorpe's vision was humanitarian by the standards of the day: rather than let debtors rot in English prisons, transport them to America where they might build new lives. Approximately 12,000 debtors were released from British prisons specifically for Georgia colonization. The colony's founding charter proclaimed it a refuge for the "worthy poor"—a charitable gloss on the reality that Britain was exporting its economic failures.

Then there were the religious radicals: Puritans, Quakers, Separatists, and other Protestant sectarians whose theological views were deemed too extreme for European society. The Puritans who founded Massachusetts Bay Colony are often romanticized as seekers of religious freedom, but the historical record is more complicated. They were expelled from England not because of persecution in the modern sense, but because their demands for radical church reform and their rejection of Anglican authority made them intolerable to the established order. They sought not freedom of religion, but freedom to impose their particular version of religious totalitarianism—which they promptly did, banishing dissenters like Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson.

The colonial sex ratio tells its own story. In early Virginia, men outnumbered women six to one. This was not the demographic profile of families seeking better lives; it was the profile of desperate single men, many bound to servitude, many criminals, few with prospects of returning home. The demographic makeup of early Pennsylvania, despite its Quaker reputation, included substantial numbers of German redemptioners—immigrants who sold themselves or their children into temporary servitude to pay for passage.

Compare this to the near-simultaneous British settlement of Australia, which began in 1788 as an explicit penal colony. Americans recoil at the "convict origin" stigma attached to Australia, yet the distinction is largely rhetorical. The American colonies preceded official penal transportation, but they served the same function: a dumping ground for Britain's unwanted populations, whether criminal, economically failed, or religiously troublesome.

This is not to impugn individual colonists, many of whom were simply poor and desperate, nor to suggest that America's founding was uniquely ignoble—all nations sanitize their origin stories. But the scale and nature of America's selective amnesia is remarkable. The national narrative has transformed criminals into pioneers, religious extremists into freedom seekers, economic failures into entrepreneurs. The myth of the "chosen nation" is not merely incomplete; it inverts the historical reality.

This inversion matters because it shapes how Americans understand their present. If the nation was founded by the virtuous, then contemporary social pathologies must represent a fall from grace, a betrayal of founding ideals. But if the nation was founded by the unwanted and unmoored, then perhaps current dysfunction is not aberration but echo—the apple not falling far from its tree.
