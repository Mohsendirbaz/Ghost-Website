The Transportation Act of 1718 formalized what had been informal practice for decades: Britain would solve its criminal justice crisis by shipping convicts to America. The numbers are staggering. Conservative estimates place transported convicts at 50,000 between 1718 and 1775. More aggressive calculations reach 120,000. Even the lower figure represents a significant proportion of early American population—in a colonial America of perhaps 2 million by 1775, convict transportation had contributed between 2.5% and 6% of the total population directly, with multigenerational descendants multiplying that impact.

These were not white-collar criminals or political prisoners romanticized in retrospect. Court records from the Old Bailey reveal the spectrum: thieves, pickpockets, burglars, forgers, prostitutes, violent offenders. The typical transported convict was young (late teens to early twenties), male, urban, and skilled in little beyond survival by illicit means. They arrived in America shackled, sold at dockside auctions to the highest bidder, and bound to seven or fourteen years of servitude depending on offense severity.

Maryland and Virginia were the primary destinations, though convicts reached all colonies. Virginia's population growth in the early 18th century was substantially driven by this influx. When colonial assemblies protested—viewing convict transportation as Britain dumping its human refuse—London overrode objections. Benjamin Franklin, writing in 1751, sarcastically suggested that America should reciprocate by shipping rattlesnakes to England, "for the sake of improving that country."

The convicts were merely the most visible element of a broader pattern. Indentured servitude—the sale of one's labor for a fixed term in exchange for passage to America—was the primary mechanism by which European poor reached the colonies. Estimates suggest 300,000 to 400,000 indentured servants arrived in British North America between 1630 and 1775. This was not slavery, technically, but the distinction was often immaterial. Servants could be bought and sold, beaten, imprisoned for infractions, and had no legal recourse against abusive masters. The mortality rate was horrific: perhaps 40% died before completing their terms.

Many indentured servants arrived through mechanisms barely distinguishable from kidnapping. The practice of "spiriting"—abducting individuals, particularly children, and transporting them for sale in America—was sufficiently common that Parliament passed laws against it. Yet enforcement was lax, and the trade continued. The term "kid-nabbing" derives from this era, referring specifically to the theft of children for American servitude.

Others came through the redemptioner system, particularly common among German immigrants in the 18th century. Families would board ships with promissory notes, agreeing to sell themselves or their children upon arrival to pay the passage. Ship captains profited handsomely. Many families were split at auction, children sold separately from parents, as buyers purchased only the labor they needed.

Then there were the religious radicals, though "radical" is the operative word. Modern Americans imagine Puritans as conservative traditionalists, but 17th-century English authorities viewed them as dangerous extremists. Their theological demands—rejection of episcopal church structure, insistence on lay control of congregations, strict enforcement of biblical law—were revolutionary. When Charles I attempted to enforce Anglican conformity, Puritans faced a choice: submit, resist and face consequences, or flee. Approximately 20,000 chose flight, establishing Massachusetts Bay Colony.

The Quakers who settled Pennsylvania were even more radical. Their rejection of church hierarchy, refusal to swear oaths, insistence on direct divine revelation, and early opposition to slavery made them pariahs in England. They were fined, imprisoned, and sometimes executed for their beliefs. George Fox, Quaker founder, spent years in prison. William Penn received Pennsylvania as payment of a debt owed to his father, but the colony became a refuge because Quakers had few other options.

Religious migration was driven by expulsion as much as attraction. The colonies were not so much a promised land as a dumping ground for theological troublemakers. And once in America, these religious communities often replicated the same intolerance they had fled. Massachusetts banished Roger Williams for advocating separation of church and state. Anne Hutchinson was expelled for suggesting direct revelation superseded clergy authority. Four Quakers were hanged on Boston Common. Puritan New England was a theocracy as strict as any European state.

The final category: the simply desperate. Economic dislocation in 17th and 18th-century Europe—enclosure movements, warfare, crop failures, population pressures—created a surplus of landless, impoverished individuals with dim prospects. For these, America represented not opportunity but desperation's last resort. The vast majority arrived as indentured servants, not free settlers. They came because remaining in Europe meant starvation or prison.

The demographic profile this created was profoundly skewed. Young, male, single, often criminal or economically marginal, with weak ties to family or community. The sex ratio in early Virginia (six men for every woman) was not the product of families seeking better lives but of a population forcibly assembled from Europe's margins. Women eventually arrived—often as indentured servants themselves, sometimes as transported convicts or prostitutes shipped to marry settlers—but the demographic imbalance persisted for generations, shaping colonial culture in ways that reverberate still.

This was the founding stock: not pioneers but deportees, not seekers of freedom but refugees from consequences, not builders of a city on a hill but the human overflow of European societies that no longer wanted them.

The apple does not fall far from its tree.
