> This page is a Persian expansion of the "Sensory Architecture Discovery" section in ARC: the core idea is that receptors in the machine-learning phase should not be designed as rigid, discrete points, but should operate continuously without the synchronization integrity among phenomenon, sensor, and learning model being ruptured. This may be achieved by absorbing information in motion without rupture (synchronization integrity is preserved) from the phenomenon under study. Fiber-like filaments capable of recording momentum at different speeds could be a revolutionary option.

## Central Thesis

The architecture of receptors in ARC can be defined on the basis of **reception continuity** and **fiber-like filaments capable of recording momentum at different speeds**: a network of sensor strands, readout paths, or sampling channels that, instead of observing the phenomenon point-wise and rigidly, create an extended, continuous, directional, and velocity-sensitive contact surface with the moving field.

Within this framework, a receptor is not merely a "measuring device"; it is a **dynamic absorption surface**. Just as, in catalysts, increasing the active cross-section can multiply the rate of absorption and reaction, in ARC's sensory architecture increasing the informational cross-section can multiply the rate at which moving, transient, and unstable patterns are absorbed.

But the key difference is this: in a catalyst, the cross-section expands to increase chemical contact; in ARC, the cross-section expands to increase **temporal–kinetic–informational** contact. The problem is therefore not merely "more resolution" but "continuity of absorption in motion"; that is, the phenomenon must be received while in motion, without the synchronization chain among phenomenon, sensor, and learning model being severed.

---

## 1. Connection with ARC's Logic

In ARC, sensor design does not begin with a fixed objective from the outset. First, phase zero — the discovery of the unconstrained envelope — reveals what information the machine-learning system actually demands: what frequency, what resolution, what spectral bandwidth, what rate of change, and what kind of spatio-temporal correlation.

The thesis of fiber-like filaments enters precisely at this point:

- If, in the learning phase, the model shows that critical information emerges in transition, motion, change of direction, vibration, slippage, or short-term turbulence, then a point-based architecture is insufficient.
- If the phenomenon under study reveals itself only in transient moments, the sensor must extend its absorption surface along the path of motion.
- If the phenomenon's momentum at different speeds carries information, the sensor must record not only position or intensity but also the impact, accelerative, and velocity-dependent effects of the phenomenon.
- If learning validity depends on synchronization, the receptor must transmit information without phase break, without sequence interruption, and without temporal gap.

ARC must therefore not view the sensor merely as an array of pixels, detectors, or samplers. The sensor must be defined as a **continuous substrate for absorbing information in motion**.

---

## 2. The Catalyst Metaphor: Increasing the Absorption Cross-Section

The principal inspiration from catalysts is that absorption performance grows manifold as the active surface increases. In sensory architecture, the equivalent of this active surface consists of:

- spatial contact surface with the phenomenon;
- temporal contact surface with the phenomenon's evolution path;
- spectral contact surface with the various states of the signal;
- kinetic contact surface with the phenomenon's direction, acceleration, rotation, and deformation;
- correlational contact surface with the dependencies among different points of the field.

In ordinary sensors, increasing the cross-section usually means "more sensors" or "higher resolution." But in this thesis, the cross-section must be **fibrous**, not merely grid-like.

Being fibrous means the receptors have paths, have extension, have short-term memory, and can follow a phenomenon while it moves. Each fiber acts like an extended absorption channel that receives the signal not as a point, but as a trace, path, or spatio-temporal slice.

---

## 3. The Principle of Non-Rupture: Preserving Synchronization Integrity

The principal advantage of the fibrous architecture is not merely greater absorption; it is greater absorption **without loss of synchronization**.

In the machine-learning phase, if the sensor produces a large volume of data but the timing, order, or phase relationship of the signals is corrupted, more data does not necessarily produce better learning. ARC must strike a balance between two needs:

1. **increasing the information-absorption surface** in order to reveal hidden structures;
2. **preserving synchronization integrity** so the model knows in what temporal and kinetic relationship each signal was recorded with the phenomenon.

Each fiber must therefore carry, in addition to the signal value, markers of synchronization as well:

- local time of reception;
- position or path of reception;
- relative direction of the phenomenon's motion;
- recorded momentum and relative velocity at the moment of reception;
- phase or order of occurrence;
- degree of confidence in sampling continuity;
- indicators of slippage, latency, or synchronization failure.

In ARC's terms, the fibrous receptor does not merely gather information; it simultaneously carries the **conditions for the validity of that information** as well.

---

## 4. Technical Advantage: Stronger Oscillations as an Upper Bound

In this thesis, stronger oscillations are not regarded as a defect; rather, they can be used as an **upper design bound**.

Sensory architecture usually tries to filter, smooth, or average oscillations. But in ARC, especially in phase zero, the goal is not to calm the world prematurely. The goal is to discover the model's true appetite for oscillation, turbulence, and motion.

Fiber-like receptors must therefore deliberately allow stronger oscillations to be recorded, because:

- strong oscillation can reveal the fatigue boundary of a module;
- strong oscillation can show which features are critical for safety or control;
- strong oscillation can expose worst-cases near the boundary earlier;
- strong oscillation can give the machine-learning model an empirical upper bound: if the model remains stable against this range, it is probably also more stable in calmer regimes.

In this sense, stronger oscillation is a design tool, not merely noise. The fibrous receptor must record oscillation in raw, synchronized form, after which ARC decides, in later phases, which part of it should enter the physical constraints.

---

## 5. Why Must Motion Enter the Sensor Directly?

In this architecture, motion is not a side effect. Motion is precisely what activates the information; especially when the phenomenon's momentum at different speeds constitutes part of the physical and learnable meaning of the signal.

If the phenomenon under study is in motion, static observation yields only an incomplete picture. The fibrous receptor must be sensitive to motion, because part of the information is revealed only through change:

- the rate of change shows which variables are close to instability;
- the direction of change shows which force or path is dominant;
- the acceleration of change shows which part is approaching a physical bound;
- velocity-dependent momentum shows what different effect the phenomenon's collision, passage, or contact produces on the reception substrate;
- the oscillation of change shows which path is harder to learn;
- a break in the continuity of change shows where synchronization is at risk.

Consequently, the sensor must not ask only "what is there?" It must ask: **what is changing, how, and is that change still synchronized with the model?**

## 6. Signal Families: Beyond the Camera

The fibrous receptor must not be understood as an extended camera. A camera mainly captures an image: it maps reflected or emitted photons onto a two-dimensional plane. But in ARC, the central problem is not the "image"; it is **recording the effect of the interaction** between the phenomenon and the reception substrate.

The base unit in this architecture is therefore not the "pixel" but the **synchronized trace of interaction**: a trace that may be constructed from scattering, sound, pressure, momentum, vibration, phase change, field disturbance, thermal gradient, fluid turbulence, impedance change, or molecular reaction. These signal families must be kept open in the learning phase, because ARC's mission is the discovery of a new design: **training first shows what kind of signal is valuable; then the receptor architecture is designed on the basis of that discovery.**

### 6.1 Scattering and Beam-Field Signals

This family comprises the kinds of change that a beam or wave undergoes after impacting, passing through, or interacting with the phenomenon:

- elastic scattering;
- inelastic scattering;
- Raman-like scattering for a molecular or structural fingerprint;
- Mie and Rayleigh scattering for particles, fog, dust, aerosols, and micro-obstacles;
- polarization change for detecting surface orientation, stress, or material anisotropy;
- phase delay and phase distortion for small geometric or density changes;
- speckle dynamics for micro-motion, roughness, vibration, or flow.

Here the sensor does not ask "what shape does the object have?"; it asks: **how did the field change after contact with the phenomenon?**

### 6.2 Acoustic and Vibrational Signals

This family is very important for the fibrous architecture, because sound and vibration inherently carry path, contact, speed, and material:

- ultrasonic reflection;
- surface acoustic wave;
- structure-borne vibration;
- resonance signature;
- acoustic Doppler shift;
- contact sound;
- emission from friction, micro-cracking, or slippage;
- pressure-wave propagation.

These signals can show at what angle, at what speed, with what density or hardness, and in what contact state the phenomenon interacted with the reception substrate.

### 6.3 Mechanical, Tactile, and Momentum Signals

This family is the part closest to the idea of "recording momentum at different speeds":

- impulse response;
- contact force;
- shear force;
- normal pressure;
- strain;
- bending;
- torsion;
- micro-slip;
- frictional traction;
- collision waveform;
- return or rebound profile;
- deformation memory.

At this level, the receptor records not merely the presence of the phenomenon, but records **how the phenomenon transferred its momentum to the reception substrate.**

### 6.4 Electromagnetic Field-Disturbance Signals

This family differs from a camera, even though light, too, is physically electromagnetic. The focus here is on field disturbance and material response:

- capacitive disturbance;
- inductive coupling;
- eddy-current response;
- microwave reflection;
- millimeter-wave scattering;
- terahertz absorption;
- radar Doppler;
- near-field disturbance;
- dielectric-constant change;
- conductivity signature.

These signals can reveal hidden structure, moisture, density, conductivity, and relative motion.

### 6.5 Thermal and Dynamic Infrared Signals

In ARC, heat must not be understood merely as a "thermal image." More important is the temporal response of heat transfer:

- thermal gradient;
- heat flux;
- transient heating or cooling response;
- change in infrared emission;
- thermal inertia;
- frictional heating;
- phase-change signature;
- convective disturbance.

The central question here is: **how does the phenomenon transmit, absorb, dissipate, or disturb heat?**

### 6.6 Fluidic and Aerodynamic Signals

If the phenomenon occurs in air, a liquid, or a moving medium, its trace remains in the medium itself:

- airflow turbulence;
- boundary-layer disturbance;
- vortex shedding;
- pressure wake;
- turbulence signature;
- drag oscillation;
- humidity or plume displacement;
- particle-flow trace.

In this case, the fibers can act like whiskers, cilia, or flow-reading filaments: they understand the phenomenon through the turbulence it creates in the medium.

### 6.7 Chemical and Molecular Signals

This family has a direct link to the catalyst metaphor. If the phenomenon leaves a molecular or chemical effect in the medium, the fibrous surface can absorb or reveal it:

- gas concentration;
- volatile compounds;
- ion concentration;
- pH;
- redox potential;
- adsorption and desorption rate;
- catalytic surface reaction;
- molecular binding events.

In this case, the receptor does not merely observe; through its active surface it enters into an absorption-based interaction with the phenomenon.

### 6.8 Magnetic and Magnetomechanical Signals

This family can be useful for detecting materials, currents, and hidden motions:

- magnetic-field distortion;
- magnetoresistance change;
- magnetoelastic response;
- ferromagnetic-material signature;
- current-induced magnetic field;
- angular field disturbance.

### 6.9 Interoceptive and Proprioceptive Signals of the Fiber Itself

If the receptor is itself fibrous, it must also record its own state:

- fiber tension;
- fiber bending;
- fiber torsion;
- internal strain wave;
- distributed stress;
- self-vibration;
- local fatigue;
- contact history.

Here the sensor does not merely sense the world; it records **how the world has changed the sensor itself.**

### 6.10 Informational and Learning-Oriented Signals

At the ARC level, some signals are not raw physical quantities but variables recorded to guide the design:

- novelty;
- surprise;
- prediction error;
- synchronization drift;
- entropy change;
- increase in mutual information;
- loss of phase coherence;
- causal latency;
- boundary violation;
- proximity to the fatigue envelope.

This family is vital for phase zero, because ARC must discover which signals actually feed the model's informational appetite and which are merely surplus data.

### The Role of All These Signals in the Discovery Mission

All of the families above must first enter the learning phase as **discovery candidates**, not as final hardware choices. In accordance with the EPU Ghost logic, the initial documents and architectures are only directional; the final design must emerge out of training, gap analysis, and the discovery of real boundaries. The correct order is therefore as follows:

1. training, in phase zero, freely experiences the wide field of signals;
2. the model shows which signal families genuinely improve understanding, control, safety, or prediction;
3. ARC builds the informational-appetite profile from these very results;
4. the receptor architecture is designed on the basis of that profile;
5. physical constraints enter only after the value of the signals has been discovered.

In short: **a camera constrains the design in advance; ARC lets training discover the type of receptor.**

## 7. Proposed Architecture: Informational Absorption Fibers

A preliminary design for this architecture could comprise the following layers:

### 7.1 The Contact-Fiber and Momentum-Recording Layer

This layer consists of sensor strands or sampling paths drawn along the probable paths of the phenomenon's motion. Its role is to increase the informational contact surface and to record the phenomenon's momentum effect at different speeds.

Characteristics:

- spatial extension instead of point-wise structure;
- directionality relative to motion;
- sensitivity to relative velocity and contact momentum;
- sensitivity to transient changes;
- the ability to record a spatio-temporal trace.

### 7.2 The Synchronization Layer

This layer exists to preserve the temporal and phase validity of the data. Each signal must be produced together with a synchronization marker.

Characteristics:

- local timestamp;
- latency index;
- continuity-break index;
- phase relationship among neighboring fibers;
- detection of synchronization slip.

### 7.3 The Controlled-Oscillation Amplification Layer

In the learning phase, this layer allows stronger oscillations to be seen as training data and a design boundary, but separates them from numerical or physical collapse.

Characteristics:

- recording the oscillation amplitude before filtering;
- labeling oscillation as an upper bound;
- distinguishing meaningless noise from structural oscillation;
- preparing the data for fatigue-envelope analysis.

### 7.4 The Meaningful-Compression Layer

Because increasing the informational cross-section can drive the data bandwidth very high, compression must be performed not on the basis of simple deletion, but on the basis of preserving learning structures.

Characteristics:

- preserving transient events;
- preserving kinetic correlations;
- reducing redundant data;
- retaining confidence and synchronization indices.

## 8. Position Within ARC's Phases

### Phase 0 — Discovering the Informational Appetite

In phase zero, the fibrous fibers and all the signal families must be activated with the least possible constraint. The goal is to determine what informational cross-section, what type of signal, and what form of interaction with the phenomenon the machine-learning model requires in its unconstrained state. Here training plays the role of design driver: if training shows that scattering, sound, momentum, field, heat, chemistry, or fluid turbulence has value, the design must follow that very discovery.

Desired outputs:

- the informational-appetite profile;
- a ranking of signal families by learning value and safety value;
- a map of intense oscillations;
- synchronization-failure points;
- the paths with the greatest learning value;
- the effective cross-section required for sufficient absorption.

### Phase 1 — The Feasibility Boundary Layer

In phase one, the physical constraints enter: power, heat, bandwidth, latency, and manufacturability. At this stage the central question is:

> How much of the fibrous cross-section can be preserved without the system leaving the physical envelope?

Desired outputs:

- minimum fiber density;
- minimum sampling rate;
- minimum synchronization precision;
- the most sensitive physical constraints;
- the points that must be either designed or eliminated.

### Phase 2 — Convergence of Training and Architecture

In phase two, the learning model and the sensor architecture are tuned bidirectionally. The model learns to work with the manufacturable cross-section; the architecture, in turn, learns which cross-section is essential for safe operation.

Convergence criteria:

- the model learns with fibrous data;
- the sensor remains manufacturable under the physical constraint;
- synchronization does not break in the worst-case motions;
- intense oscillations are preserved as an upper bound in the design envelope.

## 9. Proposed Evaluation Metrics

To operationalize this thesis, ARC must add several new metrics to its sensory architecture:

| Metric | Definition | Role in ARC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effective informational cross-section | The amount of useful sensor contact with the phenomenon across space, time, spectrum, and motion | Measuring true absorption capacity |
| Synchronization integrity | The degree to which the temporal and phase relationship between the signal and the phenomenon is preserved | Identifying data reliable for learning |
| Structural oscillation intensity | The oscillation amplitude associated with the phenomenon's motion, not random noise | Defining the upper bound for training and design |
| Velocity-dependent impulse response | The ability to record the phenomenon's effect at different speeds, including contact, passage, impact, acceleration, and change of direction | Turning motion into a learnable and synchronizable variable |
| Absorption continuity | The ability to record the phenomenon's path without breaking the sequence | Preventing data gaps during motion |
| Physical cost of the cross-section | The power, heat, bandwidth, and manufacturing complexity per unit increase in absorption surface | Entering the phase of physical constraints |
| Discovery value of a signal family | How much a signal type contributes to learning, control, safety, or uncertainty reduction in phase zero | Determining which receptors should enter the new design |
| Distance from "camera-ness" | How far the signal is from a two-dimensional image and how close to interaction effect, field, momentum, or material response | Keeping ARC oriented toward a new reception architecture rather than merely a better camera |

## 10. Implications for Machine Learning

For the machine-learning phase, this architecture has three important implications:

1. **The model receives richer but harder data.** The data are more intense, more unstable, and closer to the kinetic bounds.
2. **The model confronts worst-cases earlier.** This can make training harder, but it makes the safety envelope more realistic.
3. **Instead of learning images, the model learns path and momentum.** The fibers produce information as spatio-temporal traces and velocity-dependent responses, so the learning architecture must understand path, change, continuity, and impulse effect.
4. **The model selects which type of reception has design value.** If a signal family only yields more data but does not improve prediction error, safety, or control, it does not enter the final design; if a family such as scattering, sound, momentum, or field disturbance gives the model a real advantage, the hardware architecture should be redesigned around it.

Consequently, machine learning in ARC must not be merely convolutional or attention-based in the generic sense. It must have a mechanism for learning from **continuous informational strands**: sequences that are simultaneously spatial, temporal, kinetic, and synchronized. Here training is not a passive consumer of data; training is the **driver of the design**.

---

## 11. Risks and Kill Criteria

This thesis must be tested against hard criteria. If any of the following occur, the architecture must be revised or halted:

- increasing the informational cross-section produces only more data, not better learning;
- synchronization breaks under fast motions or intense oscillations;
- the thermal or bandwidth cost exceeds the ARC envelope;
- intense oscillations turn from a design bound into destructive noise;
- the model cannot distinguish structural oscillation from random noise;
- data compression removes important kinetic traces;
- none of the non-image signal families create meaningful discovery or safety value relative to a camera or conventional sensors;
- training cannot create a design-determining distinction among signal families.

The main kill criterion:

> If the fibrous architecture cannot simultaneously increase the absorption surface and preserve synchronization integrity, its core advantage is lost.

---

## 12. Conclusion

The proposed thesis transforms ARC's sensory architecture from a receptor array into a **continuous informational absorption fabric**. This fabric acts like fibers: extended, continuous, sensitive to motion, able to preserve the phenomenon's spatio-temporal trace, and equipped with the ability to record momentum at different speeds.

The inspiration from catalysts shows that increasing the cross-section can multiply absorption; but in ARC, the cross-section is not merely geometric. The cross-section is informational and kinetic. The receptor must, at the very moment the phenomenon moves, increase its contact surface with it and simultaneously preserve synchronization integrity.

This architecture must therefore not be reduced to a "better camera." The signal families can be scattering, sound, vibration, momentum, strain, electromagnetic field, heat, fluid turbulence, surface chemistry, magnetism, the fiber's internal state, and informational indices. All of these enter in phase zero as instruments of the discovery mission, so that training can show which path genuinely demands a new design.

The strategic advantage of this architecture is that it records stronger oscillations, before filtering, as an upper bound. In this way, the machine-learning phase confronts not a calmed version of the phenomenon, but the real boundaries of its behavior. This is exactly consistent with the spirit of ARC: first the system's true appetite is discovered; then physics, manufacturability, and safety gradually enter the design.
