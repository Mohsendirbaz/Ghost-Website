**📋 CONTENT SUMMARY:**
This chapter examines untapped institutional capacity for AI accountability in unions, community organizations, and civil society groups, paralleling religious institutional resources in the climate book. It documents both the scale of existing organizational capacity and the failure to mobilize it for AI governance.
**KEY SECTIONS:**
**7.1 Labor Union Presence and AI Exposure**
- Union membership across AI-impacted industries
- Existing grievance procedures and collective bargaining infrastructure
- Workplace AI deployment without worker input
- Organizing successes and failures around algorithmic management
**7.2 Digital Rights Organizations**
- EFF, Access Now, AI Now Institute, and similar organizations
- Existing advocacy infrastructure and expertise
- Resource constraints limiting impact
- Coalition potential with labor and community groups
**7.3 Community Organizing Capacity**
- Neighborhood associations, tenant unions, consumer groups
- Existing mobilization infrastructure
- AI harms in housing, credit, healthcare, education
- Local organizing against surveillance and algorithmic discrimination
**7.4 Professional Associations and Ethics Bodies**
- ACM, IEEE, medical associations, bar associations
- Professional ethics frameworks and enforcement mechanisms
- Industry-specific AI guidelines
- Gaps between principles and practice
**7.5 The Mobilization Failure**
- Scale of resources vs deployment for AI accountability
- Why existing infrastructure remains untapped
- Barriers to coordination across organizations
- Lessons from other technology accountability campaigns

**📊 KEY DATA POINTS & MACRO FACTS:**
**Labor Union Capacity:**
- US union membership: **14 million+** workers (10.3% of workforce)
- Tech worker organizing: **~50,000** in IATSE, CWA campaigns
- Gig worker unions: **~100,000** in emerging organizations (Rideshare Drivers United, etc.)
- Union budgets for organizing: **$2-3B annually** across major unions
- Collective bargaining coverage: **~25 million** workers (including non-members)
**Digital Rights Organizations:**
- EFF membership: **~50,000**, annual budget **$15M+**
- AI Now Institute: **~20 researchers**, **$5M** budget
- Access Now: **~200 organizations** in global coalition
- Combined digital rights NGO budgets: **<$100M globally** (vs **$10B+** tech company AI R&D)
**Community Organizing:**
- Tenant unions: **~500 organizations** nationally, **1M+ members**
- Consumer protection groups: **~200 major organizations**
- Civil rights organizations: **NAACP (2M members), ACLU (2M members)**, etc.
- Community organizing budgets: **$500M-$1B** annually across all groups
**Professional Associations:**
- ACM membership: **~100,000** computing professionals
- IEEE: **400,000+** members across all engineering
- Medical associations: **~1M** physicians with ethics frameworks
- Legal bar associations: **1.3M** attorneys with professional responsibility codes
**AI Exposure Without Voice:**
- **~50 million** US workers subject to algorithmic management (no input on systems)
- **~150 million** renters/credit applicants subject to AI screening (no recourse)
- **~70 million** students in AI-using educational systems (no participation)
- Platform workers: **~60 million globally**, **<5%** organized
**Climate Book Parallels:**
- Religious endowments ($1.2T) ≈ **Union pension funds** ($3-4T controlled by workers)
- Annual giving ($140B) ≈ **Organizing budgets** ($3-5B across labor/civil society)
- Unmobilized resources ≈ **Untapped organizing** capacity
- Tax exemption accountability ≈ **Union democracy** and member accountability

**💡 KEY INSIGHTS BY READER TYPE:**
**For Regulators:**
- Civil society capacity exists but needs activation
- Co-regulatory models can leverage organizational infrastructure
- Multi-stakeholder governance requires institutional seats at table
- Resource imbalance (civil society vs corporate AI) requires public support
- **Action:** Fund civil society AI capacity building; create formal stakeholder roles in regulatory processes; support worker organizing rights; enable community participation in AI governance
**For Academics:**
- Research opportunities: documenting organizing successes/failures, evaluating multi-stakeholder models, studying institutional mobilization
- Partnership opportunities with unions and community groups
- Translating research for organizers and policymakers
- **Action:** Partner with unions on workplace AI research; support community organizing with data/analysis; teach organizing history and strategy; join professional association AI ethics efforts
**For Workers:**
- You already have organizational infrastructure (unions, professional associations)
- Collective power can contest AI deployment decisions
- Workplace democracy includes algorithmic governance
- Mobilization requires activation not building from scratch
- **Action:** Demand AI transparency in union contracts; organize around algorithmic management; use grievance procedures for AI harms; build worker-tech coalitions
**For Organizers:**
- Existing organizational capacity vastly exceeds current AI mobilization
- Coalition potential across labor, digital rights, civil rights, consumer protection
- Successful campaigns (Google/Maven, Amazon/Rekognition) provide models
- Resource constraints real but not insurmountable
- **Action:** Build cross-movement AI accountability coalitions; organize workers facing algorithmic management; demand AI impact assessments for community; leverage existing organizational infrastructure
**For Public:**
- Institutional allies exist (unions, ACLU, consumer groups, etc.)
- Individual concerns become collective power through organization
- Participation channels available if activated
- Accountability requires mobilized civil society not just regulation
- **Action:** Join organizations working on AI accountability; support union organizing; demand organizational focus on AI issues; participate in community organizing
**For Engineers:**
- Civil society organizations are potential partners in responsible AI
- Worker organizing within tech companies can shift priorities
- Professional associations offer ethical guidance and support
- Alliance building between engineers and organized labor
- **Action:** Join ACM/IEEE AI ethics efforts; support tech worker organizing; share technical knowledge with civil society; respect worker organizing campaigns
**For Students:**
- Professional associations offer networking and ethics resources
- Student organizing can shift university AI policies
- Career choices include working with/for civil society organizations
- Organizing skills valuable alongside technical skills
- **Action:** Join student chapters of professional associations; organize on-campus around AI ethics; intern with digital rights organizations; learn labor history and organizing strategy
