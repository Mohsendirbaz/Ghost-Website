**Fiscal Impact of Major Earthquakes: A Global**

**Perspective**

**Introduction**

Earthquakes can impose massive unexpected burdens on a government’s finances, forcing significant spending on emergency response and reconstruction. In many cases, a single large earthquake can derail a national budget or require substantial external aid. This report examines how much an earthquake *adds* to a government’s fiscal-year expenditures by looking at real disaster instances across five continents. We define a “baseline” earthquake scenario in physical terms (magnitude, depth, affected radius) and compare several case studies – some less severe, some more severe, and some close to the baseline – to illustrate how financial costs can vary wildly. Throughout these examples, we also consider *timeless metrics* like the number of homes destroyed or people displaced, which help compare impact across different countries and eras without distortion from inflation or currency differences. Finally, we discuss the recent major earthquakes in Türkiye as a case study in fiscal burden.

**Defining a Baseline Earthquake Scenario**

For comparison, we establish a baseline earthquake scenario around a moderate-to-strong event in physical terms: roughly a magnitude 7.0 earthquake at a shallow depth (~10–20 km) affecting a radius of tens of kilometers around the epicenter. Such an event might heavily impact a mid-sized city or several towns, potentially displacing hundreds of thousands of people. This baseline is chosen as a median in severity –not the largest imaginable quake, but a level that historically has caused serious damage in many countries. For example, the *2023 Morocco earthquake* (magnitude 6.8, depth ~25 km) can be considered a baseline-like event: it struck 75 km from Marrakesh, killed nearly 3,000 people, and directly affected 19 provinces . In Morocco’s case, an estimated **$11.8 billion** national recovery plan was launched for rebuilding, equivalent to roughly 8% of Morocco’s GDP . We will use this baseline magnitude (~7, shallow) and impact scope as a reference point. Events physically *less destructive* (e.g. lower magnitude or remote location) and *more destructive* (higher magnitude or hitting dense urban areas) will be normalized against this baseline to highlight how fiscal costs relate to physical severity.

**Phases of Earthquake Recovery and Associated Costs**

Responding to a major earthquake usually occurs in phases, each with different cost profiles for the government:

- **Emergency Response (Immediate Relief):** In the first days and weeks, governments spend on search-and-rescue operations, medical care, temporary shelters, and emergency repairs. This phase is about saving lives and providing basic needs. The cost, while urgent, is often a smaller fraction of the total, sometimes covered by contingency funds or international humanitarian aid. For example,

1

after the 2015 Nepal quake, the government gave quick emergency cash grants to affected households for relief and funerals .

- **Short-Term Recovery (Restoration of Services):** Once life-saving operations end, focus shifts to restoring essential services (water, power, healthcare, transportation) and setting up temporary housing. Government spending during this period (weeks to months) is aimed at bringing communities to a functional state. Expenses include repairing roads, clearing debris, and strengthening unsafe structures. These costs start adding up; however, many are still interim solutions (e.g. installing tent camps or prefab housing).
- **Long-Term Reconstruction:** This is by far the most expensive phase, often spanning several fiscal years. It involves rebuilding permanent housing, schools, hospitals, and infrastructure to pre- disaster (or improved) standards. For a baseline-sized earthquake, reconstruction dominates the fiscal burden. Governments may allocate special budgets, raise taxes, or take loans for this purpose. For instance, Japan’s government committed **¥23 trillion** (about $210 billion) over a decade for reconstruction after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami

. Similarly, Italy estimated **€12–** **16 billion** would be needed to rebuild after the 2009 L’Aquila quake . Reconstruction costs often dwarf the immediate relief costs, reflecting the immense expense of safely rebuilding hundreds of thousands of homes and public buildings.

It’s important to note that these phases overlap and that the *fiscal impact* can extend over multiple budget years. Governments sometimes create multi-year recovery funds or issue special bonds. For example, after the 2011 Japan quake, the government issued reconstruction bonds and even introduced temporary tax surcharges to fund the multi-year rebuilding effort . In Türkiye, after the 1999 quake, a special“earthquake tax” was introduced (originally temporary but later made permanent) to finance disaster preparation and reconstruction . This illustrates how a single disaster can lead to fiscal policy changes lasting decades.

**Global Case Studies of Earthquake Fiscal Burdens**

To gauge the fiscal burden of earthquakes, we examine case studies from five continents. Each example notes the earthquake’s physical parameters (magnitude, depth, area, etc.), humanitarian impact (deaths, displaced population), and the estimated economic cost – especially the portion falling to the government (either directly or via publicly funded recovery). We also compare metrics like destroyed households as a proxy for scale. All costs are in nominal USD of the event year (unless noted), and we later normalize them to the baseline scenario.

**Asia: Japan’s Great East Japan Earthquake (2011)**

One of the most devastating quakes in modern history was the **2011 Great East Japan earthquake** (magnitude 9.0) and ensuing tsunami. Physically, this far exceeded our baseline: it released over 30 times more energy than a magnitude 7 quake. It struck off Japan’s Pacific coast, causing a tsunami that inundated large areas and triggered the Fukushima nuclear accident. The human toll was enormous (nearly 20,000 dead or missing) and the destruction unprecedented. The **economic cost** is estimated at around **$360 billion** – making it the *costliest natural disaster ever recorded* . The Japanese government’s fiscal burden was immense. By the 10th anniversary, the government had spent **32 trillion yen** (≈$295 billion) on recovery in the Tōhoku region, including rebuilding roads, seawalls, housing, and

2

livelihoods . This included large public works to strengthen coastal defenses (over 430 km of new seawalls) and extensive relocation and compensation programs. In addition, the government (and utility TEPCO) faced about **¥21.5 trillion** ($200 billion) in costs related to nuclear cleanup and compensation . Japan financed these costs through a combination of budget reallocations, reconstruction bonds, and a special reconstruction tax. The fiscal hit was about 4-5% of Japan’s GDP , a significant impact even for a large economy. This case shows that in a highly developed country, a mega-quake can lead to hundreds of billions in public spending – yet Japan’s strong financial system enabled it to absorb and spread these costs over several years. (By contrast, as a “timeless” metric of impact: **over 1.2 million buildings** were damaged or destroyed in this disaster, and more than 42,000 people were still displaced 10 years later due to radiation zones .)

**Europe: Italy’s L’Aquila Earthquake (2009)**

In Europe, a notable example is the **2009 L’Aquila earthquake** in central Italy. This quake was physically *less severe* than our baseline – magnitude 6.3 and relatively shallow – but it struck directly under the medieval city of L’Aquila, causing the collapse of many buildings (including modern apartments and historic structures). Over 300 people died and about 66,000 were left homeless . The *financial impact* was very high relative to the quake’s size. Total damage was estimated around **$16 billion**

. The Italian government initially set aside roughly **€12 billion** (≈ $15 billion) for reconstruction of the Abruzzo region

, and subsequent allocations climbed to about €17.5 billion over the next decade . This money went into rebuilding tens of thousands of homes, restoring infrastructure, and reinforcing buildings against future quakes. One challenge Italy faced was the *duration* of reconstruction – even 15 years later, the rebuild of L’Aquila’s city center is not fully complete (by 2025 it was ~78% funded and ~88% of projects completed)

. The fiscal burden has stretched across many annual budgets, illustrating how even a moderate-magnitude earthquake in a densely populated area can create a long-term financial commitment. *(A photo of the provincial government office (“Palazzo del Governo”) in L’Aquila, ruined by the quake, became a symbol of the destruction.* *) The L’Aquila event underscores that building vulnerability and cultural heritage restoration can drive costs up: many of the damaged structures were unreinforced masonry, requiring expensive rebuilding, and Italy had to finance both new housing for residents and the painstaking restoration of historic monuments – an effort still ongoing.

**Americas: Chile’s Maule Earthquake (2010)**

South America’s most significant recent event was the **2010 Maule, Chile earthquake** (magnitude 8.8) and tsunami. This quake was *more physically powerful* than our baseline and affected a wide area including several cities (Concepción, Talcahuano, etc.). Casualties were over 500 dead – far lower than similar quakes in less prepared countries – but infrastructure damage was extensive. Approximately **370,000 housing units** were damaged, with over a million people briefly displaced . The Chilean government quickly performed damage assessments and estimated the reconstruction cost at about **$30 billion**, roughly **18% of Chile’s GDP** . This included rebuilding or repairing over 220,000 homes (the government took responsibility for housing assistance to low- and middle-income families), as well as hundreds of schools, hospitals, roads, and bridges . Fortunately, Chile had prepared fiscal buffers; it had a national rainy-day fund from copper revenues and was able to finance much of the rebuilding domestically . The government still implemented measures to cover the costs, such as a temporary tax hike on large companies to raise funds . Chile’s economy experienced a sharp, but short-lived, setback – the economy contracted in the immediate aftermath but then rebounded with the reconstruction boom. The fiscal strain (directly ~$30 billion) was large for a middle-income country, but Chile’s prudent financial management

3

prior to the disaster meant it could handle the burden without external bailouts. This case demonstrates how even an earthquake *close to the high end of severity* can be managed if a government has strong disaster funds and plans. (It’s worth noting that Chile’s strict building codes kept destruction lower than it could have been – a lesson in how investing in resilience can reduce fiscal pain later. For comparison, the slightly smaller 2010 Haiti earthquake caused an estimated $8–14 billion in damage in a much poorer, less-prepared nation – essentially wiping out Haiti’s GDP and requiring an international donor effort, since the Haitian government alone could not bear the cost.)

**Africa: Morocco’s High Atlas Earthquake (2023)**

Africa sees fewer large earthquakes, but in **September 2023 Morocco** experienced a magnitude 6.8 quake in the High Atlas Mountains – an event we are using as akin to the *baseline scenario* in this analysis. It struck rural areas of Al Haouz province and parts of Marrakesh, killing 2,900+ people and injuring over 18,000 . Entire villages in the mountains were devastated, with traditional adobe houses collapsing. The geographic extent was broad (19 provinces affected) but the most severe damage was concentrated in less-densely built areas. Morocco’s government, with World Bank support, performed a rapid damage assessment. Within weeks, authorities announced an **$11.8 billion** recovery and reconstruction plan . This plan includes rebuilding about 50,000 destroyed homes (many to be reconstructed with quake-resistant materials), compensating victims’ families, and strengthening infrastructure like roads and water systems in the region. To finance this, Morocco tapped an existing national disaster resilience fund and secured about $300 million in immediate financing from a pre-arranged World Bank facility

. The $11.8 billion cost is about 7–8% of Morocco’s GDP, a significant one-time shock to the fiscal system . The government is likely to spread expenditures over several years, prioritizing hardest-hit communities first. This earthquake’s financial burden – while heavy for Morocco – is being somewhat mitigated by international loans and Morocco’s own reserves. It illustrates a “median” case: a baseline-magnitude quake in a middle-income country still leads to on the order of $10 billion of reconstruction needs. Notably, in this disaster, around **300,000 people** were directly affected and **tens of thousands of houses** were destroyed or rendered unsafe . Those figures help explain the price tag. Compared to richer countries, the cost in dollars is lower because of simpler rural construction, but relative to the size of Morocco’s economy, it represents a very large fiscal challenge.

**Oceania: New Zealand’s Canterbury Earthquakes (2010–2011)**

In 2010 and 2011, the Canterbury region of New Zealand (including the city of Christchurch) was struck by a sequence of powerful quakes, the worst being a shallow **M6.2 quake in February 2011** right under Christchurch. Though the magnitude was smaller than the baseline, the intensity in the city was extreme (MMI XI) due to the shallow depth (5 km) and soil conditions . 185 people lost their lives and thousands were injured in that disaster

. The compounded damage of the 2010 and 2011 quakes effectively turned into a single reconstruction effort. In terms of economic cost, the **2010–2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence** caused an estimated **$40–50 billion USD** in damage (about NZ$52 billion) . This is a huge number for a country of New Zealand’s size – roughly 20% of its annual GDP. However, a large portion of these losses was covered by insurance. New Zealand’s government had a public Earthquake Commission (EQC) scheme that insured home damages, as well as high private insurance penetration. Insurance payouts for property claims were around **$38 billion NZD** (~$25 billion USD) . Even so, the government bore significant costs: it had to fund infrastructure repairs (water, sewer, roads) and provide support for uninsured losses. The central government also injected funds to backstop the EQC when claims exceeded reserves. For Christchurch City Council alone, direct cost to repair local infrastructure topped

4

NZ$2–3 billion, partly funded by central government grants . The national government created a Canterbury Earthquake Recovery Authority to manage the rebuild, and over multiple budgets allocated several billion NZD to reconstruction projects and city revitalization. In sum, New Zealand’s fiscal burden was substantial but manageable through a combination of disaster reserves, reinsurance recoveries, and some debt. The Canterbury case shows that even a smaller-magnitude quake can impose large fiscal costs if it hits a populated area: over **100,000 homes** needed repairs or rebuilding in greater Christchurch, and entire neighborhoods had to be abandoned due to land subsidence (the government purchased over 7,000 red-zoned properties, a cost by itself of NZ$1+ billion). Because of strong financial preparedness (insurance and a relatively robust economy), New Zealand was able to handle an event that cost on the order of 1/5 of its GDP, although the recovery took many years.

*(Oceania has also seen other costly quakes, such as the 1995 Kobe earthquake in Japan’s region – Asia-Pacific –which cost about $200 billion* *, and the 2011 Christchurch event discussed above. We focus on Christchurch as the primary Oceania example, noting that Japan’s Kobe is another instructive case: Kobe (M6.9) caused ~$100–$200 billion damage in a single city* *, highlighting again how urban quakes drive costs.)*

**Normalizing Financial Costs to the Baseline**

Using our baseline scenario (≈M7 shallow, affecting a moderate population), we can compare and **normalize** the fiscal burdens:

- **Baseline (e.g. Morocco 2023, ~M6.8):** Cost ≈ **$12 billion**, hundreds of thousands homeless. (Normalize as 1× baseline unit.)
- **Less Destructive Physically (smaller magnitude or remote):** For example, Italy’s L’Aquila 2009 was M6.3 (much less energy than M7) but cost ~$16 billion. That is **~1.3× baseline cost**, showing that even a weaker quake can impose a larger bill if it strikes a vulnerable city. Conversely, Nepal’s 2015 quake (M7.8) was physically larger but in a poorer rural setting – its rebuild cost (~$7–8 billion) was actually *below* our baseline in USD, yet extremely high relative to Nepal’s GDP (about 30% of GDP)

. Thus, “less destructive” in physical terms doesn’t always mean less fiscal burden – it depends on what is hit.

- **More Destructive Physically (higher magnitude or urban):** Japan 2011 (M9.0) was off the charts – ~30× the baseline magnitude energy – and its ~$295 billion government spending is about **25×** our baseline cost . Chile 2010 (M8.8) was ~5× energy of baseline and cost ~$30 billion (≈2.5× baseline). Türkiye 2023 (M7.8) had a larger affected population than baseline and multiple cities hit; its damage is estimated ~$100 billion+, roughly **8–9× baseline**

. New Zealand 2011 (M6.2 but a direct city hit) cost ~$40 billion, about **3× baseline**. The variability is large.

These comparisons show that *financial cost is not a linear function of magnitude*. A slightly larger quake can cost an order of magnitude more if it hits a dense or high-value area. Other factors influencing fiscal impact include: the country’s wealth (rich countries have more expensive property to rebuild, but also more resources), building standards (poor construction means more destruction and thus more to rebuild), insurance coverage (high insurance can shift costs off government budgets), and disaster response efficiency. The **number of buildings destroyed** is a particularly telling metric: for instance, the twin earthquakes in Türkiye 2023 caused **over 298,000 buildings** to collapse or require demolition – an astounding figure that directly explains why the costs there soared (in baseline terms, that’s perhaps 10× a

5

baseline scenario’s destruction). On the other hand, if an earthquake hits mostly uninhabited mountains, the fiscal cost can be negligible even if the magnitude is high.

In summary, one *baseline-unit* earthquake (~M7, moderate impact zone) might typically cost on the order of 5–10% of a nation’s GDP in reconstruction. Less severe events may cost a few billion (or be absorbed locally), whereas the most severe can cost a quarter of a trillion dollars. The global data indicates governments often shoulder a large share of these costs – in developing countries, usually through international aid and loans, and in developed countries, through budget reallocations, emergency funds, or new taxes.

**Case Focus: The Fiscal Burden of Recent Earthquakes in Türkiye**

Finally, it’s instructive to consider how much trouble the **recent major earthquakes in Türkiye (Turkey)** have created for the government’s finances. In February 2023, southern Turkey was struck by a pair of massive earthquakes (M7.8 and M7.5) hours apart. This disaster closely fits a *worst-case baseline multiplier*: very strong quakes, shallow depth, and a wide impact area including multiple cities and 11 provinces. The human toll exceeded 50,000 deaths in Turkey (and additional in Syria) . From a fiscal perspective, the numbers are staggering. The World Bank’s rapid assessment in late February 2023 estimated **$34.2 billion** in direct physical damage in Turkey (about 4% of GDP) . However, this was considered a **floor** for costs:“recovery and reconstruction costs will be much larger, potentially twice as large” . Indeed, by March 2023, the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) indicated that **Turkey’s damage would exceed $100 billion** . This $100 billion+ figure (≈10% of GDP) is being used for donor conferences and planning

. It implies a reconstruction effort on the scale of rebuilding an entire region: more than **500,000 new housing units** are needed (the Turkish government reported over 298,000 buildings destroyed or slated for demolition, plus many more with lighter damage) .

Such an enormous project has immediate and long-term fiscal implications. In the short term, Turkey’s government increased spending for emergency relief, provided cash aid to millions of displaced residents, and offered tax deferrals and other financial relief in the quake zone. The government also faced revenue losses from the affected region’s economic disruption. One analysis noted that government spending on reconstruction would sharply boost Turkey’s budget in 2023, partly offsetting the GDP contraction from the disaster

. In the longer term, funding the reconstruction (over $100 billion) will require a mix of reallocating budget resources, raising loans, and seeking foreign aid. Turkey has already appealed for international grants and has tapped some foreign credit. There is precedent: after the 1999 Izmit earthquake, Turkey’s economic growth fell by about 3.3% and the government had to manage billions in rebuilding costs . The government introduced the special “earthquake tax” then (on telecommunications) which, over two decades, raised the equivalent of **$4.5–$37 billion** (depending on currency value assumptions) . Critics in Turkey have questioned whether those funds were effectively used for disaster preparedness – a debate reignited by the 2023 quakes.

For the **2023 quakes**, Turkey’s fiscal burden can be summarized as follows: an initial **emergency aid package of $1.9 billion** was announced within days (including the release of contingency funds) ; the **total reconstruction bill** is expected to be on the order of **$100 billion** (the government’s own preliminary figure, supported by international experts, was already ~$100 billion by March

). This will likely be spent over several years, but even if spread over, say, five years, it could add an extra $20 billion per year –roughly 2.5% of Turkey’s annual GDP – in public spending on top of normal budgets. In practical terms, Turkey’s 2023 budget deficit will widen substantially due to quake spending, and additional borrowing is expected. The situation in Syria (also hit by the quake) adds complexity; Syria’s recovery needs are estimated

6

around $14–15 billion, but that burden falls largely on international aid due to Syria’s economic state . For Turkey, being a larger economy, the government is the key actor for rebuilding. Despite Turkey’s efforts to insure property (a compulsory earthquake insurance pool exists), insurance will cover only a fraction of the losses, so the state must fill the gap. The 2023 Turkey-Syria earthquake disaster illustrates how a single event can present a fiscal shock on the order of a **major war or financial crisis**, demanding a sustained and organized fiscal response.

**Conclusion**

Major earthquakes pose a serious risk not just to lives and buildings but also to government finances. Our survey of events across continents shows that a shallow ~M7 quake – our baseline – can easily impose a **double-digit billion dollar** reconstruction cost (often 5–10% of a nation’s GDP for middle-income countries). Less severe quakes can still burden budgets if they hit vulnerable urban areas, whereas more severe quakes can overwhelm even wealthy nations’ resources. Critically, the financial burden is not strictly proportional to physical metrics like magnitude; instead, it’s mediated by factors such as population exposure, building resilience, economic development, and preparedness. For example, Japan and New Zealand, with strong building codes and insurance, incurred huge absolute costs but were able to finance them without derailing their economies. Poorer countries like Nepal or Haiti saw “smaller” dollar losses that were catastrophic relative to their size, requiring global aid. Every case, however, reinforces that governments must plan for *post-disaster expenditures*: establishing emergency funds, obtaining contingency credit lines or insurance, and enacting mechanisms (like Turkey’s earthquake tax or Japan’s reconstruction bonds) to spread the cost over time.

In answering “how much does an earthquake add burden to the fiscal year of a government,” one must consider both the **immediate fiscal hit** – often a sudden spike in spending on relief and temporary living needs in the current year – and the **multi-year burden** of reconstruction appropriations. A large earthquake can transform a government’s fiscal priorities for years. As seen with Turkey’s 2023 disaster, the government will be budgeting for this earthquake well into the future, effectively adding a whole new spending category that rivals its pre-disaster education or healthcare budgets in size. The *range* of fiscal burden is wide: at the low end, a moderate quake might add a few percent of GDP worth of spending; at the high end, the worst quakes have demanded 10% or more of GDP in public costs

. Globally, disaster analysts note that **governments typically shoulder a major share of post-disaster costs**, either directly or by backing loans to rebuild . This is why investment in disaster risk reduction is often emphasized– every dollar spent on strengthening buildings or planning better can save many dollars in reconstruction.

In sum, a baseline earthquake can burden a government with expenditures on the order of **$10 billion (baseline)**, and depending on circumstances, this burden can scale down to a few billion or up to hundreds of billions. The cases from Japan, Italy, Chile, Morocco, New Zealand, and Turkey collectively demonstrate that while the physical earth may shake locally, the *financial shockwaves* are felt in the national treasury and can reshape public finance for years after the ground stops shaking. Governments around the world are increasingly aware of this and are integrating disaster contingencies into fiscal planning – because when the next big earthquake strikes, the bills start coming due immediately and continue long after the headlines fade.
