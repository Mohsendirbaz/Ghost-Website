**Standardized Writing Grading Rubrics**

**Widely-used Academic Rubrics:** Major standardized tests employ detailed rubrics to ensure consistent essay evaluation. For example, **TOEFL iBT** and **GRE Analytical Writing** use *holistic* scoring on fixed scales (TOEFL Writing 0–5, GRE 0–6) based on overall writing quality . In contrast, tests like the **SAT Essay** (discontinued in 2021) and **IELTS Writing** use *analytic* rubrics with multiple dimensions. The SAT essay was scored in three domains (Reading, Analysis, Writing) each on a 2–8 scale

, while IELTS writing assigns **four** equally weighted category scores (Task Response, *Coherence and Cohesion*, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range/Accuracy) averaged into a 0–9 band .

**Holistic vs. Analytic Scoring:** *Holistic* scoring means a single overall score is given based on general impression, whereas *analytic* scoring involves separate subscores for defined criteria (content, organization, language, etc.) . Holistic rubrics (e.g. GRE, TOEFL) allow quick judgments of overall effectiveness, but analytic rubrics (IELTS, SAT) provide diagnostic feedback by rating distinct traits (e.g. idea development, coherence, grammar)

. Some tests blend approaches: TOEFL and GRE use holistic scores but their scoring guides still describe key traits like development, organization, and language control . IELTS is fully analytic: examiners give 4 sub-scores (each 25% weight) for the specified categories

. Analytic scoring is often preferred for instructional settings because it pinpoints strengths/weaknesses (e.g. strong content but weak coherence) , whereas holistic scoring is efficient for large-scale scoring but more subjective in weighing different aspects .

**Common Rubric Criteria:** Despite format differences, rubrics tend to evaluate similar aspects. Typical dimensions include: **Content & Task Fulfillment** (addressing the topic, quality of ideas), **Organization/ Coherence** (logical structure and flow), **Support/Development** (examples, reasoning), **Language Use** (grammar, vocabulary, style), and sometimes **Mechanics** (spelling, punctuation). For instance, the TOEFL independent writing rubric explicitly mentions a top essay *“is well organized and well developed…displays unity, progression, and coherence”* , whereas a low-scoring essay may show *“inadequate organization or connection of ideas”* . IELTS Band 7+ descriptors require *“logically organizes information and ideas; there is clear progression throughout”*, while Band 4 or below essays *“are not arranged coherently… no clear progression”* . The **GRE** scoring guide similarly notes that a 5–6 essay *“is well focused and organized”*, whereas a 2–3 essay has *“lack of organization”* or coherence problems . Even the SAT writing domain stressed organization: a top essay *“demonstrates a deliberate and highly effective progression of ideas…cohesive”*, while a weak response *“demonstrates little cohesion”* (as noted in score explanations) . Across systems, **coherence/organization is a core criterion**, along with development of ideas, linguistic correctness, and task relevance.

**Theoretical Frameworks for Text Appraisal**

Academic writing assessment is informed by classical argumentation and learning theory frameworks:

- **Toulmin’s Model of Argumentation:** Stephen Toulmin’s schema breaks an argument into logical components: *Claim, Grounds (Evidence), Warrant*, with possible *Backing, Qualifier,* and *Rebuttal*. This model is used to appraise the strength of arguments in analytic writing. Evaluators may check if an essay has a clear claim, relevant evidence, and sound warrants linking evidence to the claim. Research has shown that **presence and structure of Toulmin elements correlate with argument** **quality** in student essays

. For instance, essays containing well-supported claims (grounds + warrant) tend to receive higher quality ratings . However, recent studies suggest it’s not just having the elements, but how well they’re integrated – the relationships between claims and support, and the logical flow (e.g. appropriate rebuttals, qualifiers) are strong predictors of essay quality

. Incorporating Toulmin’s framework in evaluation encourages a focus on argumentative coherence: does the essay present a claim and systematically support it? Some analytic rubrics (especially for persuasive writing) explicitly reward the presence of a thesis and supporting arguments, essentially reflecting Toulmin criteria in educational terms (e.g. a category for *Argument/* *Reasoning* or *Evidence*).

- **Bloom’s Taxonomy of Cognitive Skills:** Bloom’s taxonomy classifies cognitive complexity from lower-order to higher-order thinking: *Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis,* and *Evaluation* . In writing appraisal, this can manifest in judging the **depth of analysis** or critical thinking in an essay. For example, an essay that merely recalls facts (Knowledge level) would score lower on content quality than one that analyzes or evaluates concepts (Analysis/Evaluation levels). Some grading guides prompt raters to reward essays demonstrating higher-order thinking (e.g. evaluating different perspectives or creating a novel argument). Bloom’s framework also helps in designing prompts and rubrics – an “excellent” essay might be expected to perform analysis and evaluation of the topic (higher Bloom levels), not just describe or summarize (lower levels). Rubrics for advanced placement or graduate-level writing often implicitly use Bloom’s levels, expecting synthesis of ideas and critical evaluation for top scores . Thus, an essay’s position on Bloom’s scale (e.g. showing creative synthesis vs. basic understanding) can be a qualitative appraisal dimension.
- **SOLO Taxonomy:** The Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes (SOLO) taxonomy provides another lens for evaluating the complexity of student responses. It describes levels of understanding in ascending order: *Pre-structural (irrelevant or no understanding), Uni-structural (one aspect understood),* *Multi-structural (several aspects but unintegrated), Relational (aspects integrated into a coherent whole),* *Extended Abstract (generalizes to broader principles)*

. In assessing writing, a higher-quality essay might be seen as *relational or extended abstract* – it not only covers multiple points but connects them and possibly extrapolates to new insights. For example, an analytic text at the extended abstract level will not just make multiple points, but will relate them to each other and to a larger context or theory (showing a deep, transferable understanding) . Educators sometimes use SOLO-based rubrics, where an essay that merely lists reasons would be multi-structural (score in mid-range), while one that weaves reasons into a single convincing argument with reflection or broader implications would be extended abstract (high score). SOLO thus provides a structured way to judge **coherence and depth** of understanding in writing – rewarding integration and abstraction in content.

2

- **Educational Writing Scales and Traits:** Beyond exam rubrics, writing is often assessed via trait- based scales from educational psychology. A prominent example is the *“Six Traits of Writing”* model (ideas, organization, voice, word choice, sentence fluency, conventions), which is used in many K-12 contexts. This trait rubric explicitly includes **Ideas (content)** and **Organization**, aligning with coherence and argument structure, as well as language aspects (word choice, fluency) and correctness (conventions). Another is the APA Taskforce’s rubrics or the *College Board’s AP History DBQ* *rubric*, etc., which often have separate criteria for *thesis/claim*, *evidence*, *analysis*, *organization*, *style*. These scales reflect a blend of cognitive frameworks (rewarding analysis/synthesis) and classical rhetoric (argument and coherence). In higher education, **writing assessment rubrics** may incorporate constructs like *critical thinking* (akin to Bloom’s higher levels) or *structure of argument* (akin to Toulmin), along with writing mechanics . Overall, theoretical frameworks ensure that essay appraisal is not just about surface features but also about the quality of reasoning and understanding demonstrated.

**Coherence and Cohesion in Appraisal**

**Coherence as a Scoring Dimension:** Coherence – the logical connectedness of ideas – is a key factor in text quality and is explicitly mentioned in many rubrics. Human raters typically judge coherence through an essay’s organization, clarity of progression, and use of connecting devices. For example, TOEFL raters look for *“unity, progression, and coherence”* in awarding top scores . IELTS examiners have a dedicated *Coherence and Cohesion* category: a band 9 uses cohesion so well it’s “invisible” (no awkwardness), and even band 6 requires information to be *“arranged coherently with a clear overall progression”*

. Essays that are disorganized or jump between ideas will be penalized (e.g. IELTS band 5 mentions “lack of overall progression” and inadequate use of cohesive devices ). Thus, in manual grading, coherence is often evaluated via rubric descriptors (logical flow, paragraphing, transitions).

**Manual vs. Automated Coherence Evaluation:** Traditionally, coherence is judged qualitatively by human readers. However, *automated* approaches have been developed to consistently annotate or score coherence. **Coh-Metrix** is a notable tool that provides quantitative indices of cohesion and coherence for texts. Coh-Metrix analyzes a text on multiple levels (words, syntax, semantics) and computes dozens of measures –from basic counts (word frequency, sentence length) to advanced *cohesion indices* (e.g. overlapping content words between sentences, use of pronoun reference, connectives) . Some Coh-Metrix indices approximate coherence by checking how well sentences and paragraphs link to each other (for instance, **lexical overlap** or semantic similarity between adjacent sentences is assumed to reflect local cohesion). High-coherence writing tends to have clear referential ties and logical transitions, which such metrics attempt to capture. Studies have used Coh-Metrix features to predict human judgments of essay quality; for example, certain cohesion metrics significantly distinguish high- vs low-rated essays . Another research tool, the **entity grid model**

, represents the pattern of how “entities” (nouns) appear across sentences –coherent texts maintain smoother referential continuity of entities. This model (Barzilay & Lapata, 2005) and its extensions (entity graphs, etc.) have been used to automate coherence scoring by detecting disrupted patterns in low-coherence essays .

**Annotation Practices:** In research settings, coherence is sometimes annotated on a numeric scale. For example, the **Grammarly Corpus of Discourse Coherence (GCDC)** collected **human ratings of coherence** for hundreds of texts (on a Likert scale) to create a evaluation benchmark . Expert annotators rated each text’s overall coherence, and these scores serve as ground truth to train/test automated coherence models

. Manual annotation guidelines for coherence often define it in terms of *logical organization, presence of*

3

*transitions, absence of non-sequiturs*. Inter-rater reliability for coherence can be a challenge – raters need training to apply coherence criteria consistently. The GCDC project reported that with clear guidelines, expert raters achieved reasonably high agreement on coherence judgments (difficult texts with jumps in topic received low scores, well-structured texts high scores) . Some standardized assessments also implicitly annotate coherence; for instance, TOEFL and GRE have multiple raters whose score differences reflect implicit coherence judgments, and discrepancies might trigger a third rater. In the case of **ASAP** (described below), although holistic, many prompts include coherence in their scoring rubrics for human graders , and automated systems trained on those scores indirectly learn a notion of coherence.

On the **automation side**, tools like Coh-Metrix are often used in **hybrid** assessment: an instructor might run student essays through Coh-Metrix to get objective signals (e.g. low cohesion indices could flag a potentially incoherent essay for closer review). In research, automated **coherence scores** (from models or indices) have been included as features in AES systems to improve scoring accuracy . For example, Somasundaran et al. (2014) designed features to specifically score *organization/coherence* in essays , and Persing et al. (2010) predicted an “organization” score for essays using discourse features. These efforts treat coherence as a separable dimension that can be automatically rated with reasonable reliability, mirroring the way a human would rate coherence as one part of writing quality.

**Key Datasets for Essay Grading Research**

To develop and evaluate appraisal methods, researchers rely on corpora of essays with human annotations (scores or labels). Notable datasets include:

- **ASAP (Automated Student Assessment Prize) Essay Corpus:** A widely used public dataset originally from a 2012 Kaggle competition. It contains ~13,000 essays from U.S. middle and high school students across 8 prompts . Each essay was written on a specific prompt (ranging from persuasive letters to literary analysis) and was **scored by two human raters** on either a holistic scale or an analytic rubric, depending on the prompt

. Final scores per essay were obtained via either averaging or adjudication – for some prompts the two raters’ scores were summed or the higher score taken, and for others, rubric sub-scores were combined . Most prompts have a holistic score (e.g. 1–6 points)

, while two prompts included trait scores like *content, organization, style* which were then summed . The **inter-rater reliability** in this corpus is high by design (generally weighted kappa ~0.7–0.8 between human raters, as required for scoring quality) – indeed, competition evaluations used Quadratic Weighted Kappa comparing automated vs. human scores, mirroring human-human agreement

. ASAP has become a **benchmark** for Automated Essay Scoring research ; many AES models are trained and tested on it. An extension called **ASAP++** provided *attribute-level annotations* (scores for individual rubric dimensions like organization) for essays to enable finer-grained modeling

. This dataset’s prompts also came with **scoring** **guides** for human graders, delineating what constitutes a high or low score in terms of idea development, organization, etc., which has allowed researchers to align automated features with human criteria.

- **TOEFL11 Corpus:** A corpus of 12,100 essays from TOEFL (Test of English as a Foreign Language) examinees, balanced across 11 native language backgrounds (1,100 essays per L1) . This dataset was created by ETS primarily for *Native Language Identification* research, but it also includes **proficiency labels** for each essay. Specifically, essays are labeled **low**, **medium**, or **high** proficiency, derived from the original TOEFL writing scores (two human rater scores on a 5-point scale, combined

4

and then collapsed into three bands) . Thus, while not offering exact numeric scores, TOEFL11 provides a coarse grading that correlates with essay quality. It’s useful for studying how linguistic features vary with proficiency and has been used in automated scoring experiments as well . TOEFL11 is an *exam-based* dataset, so prompts and time constraints were standardized, and annotation guidelines (the official TOEFL rubric) ensured consistent human scoring. Because essays also span many L1 backgrounds, TOEFL11 has been valuable for researching fairness and bias in scoring (ensuring an AES model doesn’t just detect the author’s native language or vocabulary biases, but truly evaluates quality).

- **Grammarly’s GCDC (Corpus of Discourse Coherence):** This is a specialized dataset focusing on **discourse coherence** in real-world texts. It contains four genres of writing (Yahoo forum answers, Yelp restaurant reviews, emails, and blog posts) with a total of 1,200 documents, each **rated for** **coherence by experts on a 1–5 scale** . Unlike exam essays, these are naturally occurring texts (with varying topics and informality). The GCDC corpus was introduced to evaluate how well automated coherence algorithms perform on *noisy, real-world data* . Each text in GCDC has multiple human ratings which were averaged to a coherence score; annotation guidelines were developed to train raters on what “coherent” vs “incoherent” writing looks like (for example, an email that jumps between unrelated points would score low). The creators reported fairly robust inter-rater alignment given the subjective nature of coherence

. GCDC’s importance lies in providing a ground truth for coherence: it has been used to benchmark models like entity-grid, lexical cohesion models, and newer neural coherence models . In the *first large-scale evaluation*, Lai & Tetreault (2018) found that even top algorithms had much lower accuracy on GCDC than on artificial tasks, highlighting the challenge of modeling coherence in the wild . For our purposes, GCDC offers a way to test a coherence-focused metric (like our model) against human judgments.

- **ICLE (International Corpus of Learner English):** A classic corpus comprising **6,000+ essays by EFL (English as a Foreign Language) university students** from various mother-tongue backgrounds

. Compiled by Granger et al. (2009), ICLE is used to study learner writing patterns. The essays are typically 500-1000 words argumentative papers written under semi-formal conditions (often as part of exams or assignments in different countries). ICLE is not a *single assessment* – prompts and scoring conditions vary – so it doesn’t come with a unified score for each essay. However, many ICLE essays have been marked by teachers, and some subsets include scores or error annotations. In research, ICLE is often used for qualitative analyses (e.g. how do French vs. Chinese learners structure their essays) and for training AES models that generalize across diverse writing styles. For example, it has been used to examine how cohesion devices differ by proficiency or L1, which ties into perceived coherence. The lack of standardized scores in ICLE is a limitation, so newer corpora (like TOEFL11 or the **FCE corpus** from Cambridge exams, which contains exam scores) are sometimes preferred for scoring research . Nonetheless, ICLE’s large scale and rich variation make it a valuable reference for understanding what features (vocabulary richness, cohesive device use, error rates) correlate with stronger vs. weaker ESL writing.

- **Other Notable Corpora:** The **Kaggle ASAP–Summarization (ASAP 2.0)** corpus (2022) expanded on ASAP with ~24,000 argumentative essays, each responding to a source text (a more complex task)

. Each essay was scored for overall writing quality, and the dataset includes richer demographic data. There’s also the **FCE dataset** (First Certificate in English exam writings) with around 1,244 scripts graded by examiners on detailed scales, and the **GLEC** and **AEON** corpora for Japanese learners, etc. Additionally, the **GCDC** is complemented by smaller coherence datasets (e.g. essays

5

annotated for coherence breaks in research by T. Crossley and D. McNamara). When building our system, we may leverage such corpora to validate that our appraisal metrics (especially coherence) correlate with these human-labeled scores. We should also consult **annotation guidelines** published with these datasets (e.g. the TOEFL11 documentation provides the rating scale definitions

, and ASAP’s prompt-specific rubrics are available in the competition materials). Understanding the **inter-rater reliability** in these sets is crucial: for instance, the ASAP essays had two raters and used a third adjudicator if scores disagreed too much, ensuring a gold standard for model training

. Similarly, knowing that TOEFL11’s high/medium/low is based on averaging two raters gives confidence in its labels . In summary, these datasets provide both the **data for machine learning** and the **benchmark for human standards** that we need to integrate classical appraisal into our system.

**Automated Essay Scoring (AES) Systems and Models**

In academic assessment, **Automated Essay Scoring** systems are increasingly used alongside human raters. These systems range from traditional statistical models to modern NLP-based models:

- **Project Essay Grade (PEG):** The first AES system (Page, 1966), which pioneered using simple proxies for writing quality (like essay length, vocabulary measures, etc.). PEG established that features like word count, average word length, and typographical errors could predict human scores to an extent.

Modern descendants of PEG still consider such surface features as part of a broader feature set.

- **ETS** e-rater***: Developed by Educational Testing Service, e-rater is one of the most influential AES*** ***engines. It uses a* feature-based* approach grounded in NLP and decades of research. The e-** **rater v2+ engine evaluates an essay on multiple dimensions:** grammar, usage, mechanics, style, vocabulary, organization, and discourse coherence **. Specifically, e-rater extracts features such** **as: grammatical error counts (subject-verb agreement, etc.), word choice sophistication,** **redundancy, sentence variety, and indicators of organization (e.g. presence of thesis** **statement, logical paragraph breaks)** **. Notably, ETS reports that e-rater includes** **measures of *discourse coherence* – for example, it checks for logical connectors and transition** **words, and can flag if an essay seems to lack logical flow**

**. E-rater is used operationally to** **score millions of essays (including as a second scorer on TOEFL and GRE). It often achieves** **human-level agreement: *“the e-rater engine often agrees with human ratings more strongly than***  ***human raters agree among themselves.”*** **. However, to ensure validity, high-stakes tests use a** **hybrid approach: if e-rater’s score disagrees too much with a human rater, a second human** **score is used instead**

**. E-rater’s success comes from aligning features with** constructs in the rubric** – an Evidence-Centered Design approach means each feature (like a cohesion count) is intended to evidence a writing ability . For coherence, for instance, e-rater might include a feature counting how many discourse connectives (“however,” “therefore,” etc.) are used, or whether ideas are repeated without development (which might indicate poor cohesion). Research confirms that adding discourse structure features improves AES accuracy . E-rater has been continually updated (e.g., adding rhetoric-related features) to cover more subtle aspects like argumentation. It’s a closed commercial system, but many academic AES models emulate its feature set.

- **Pearson’s Intelligent Essay Assessor (IEA):** IEA takes a different approach, focusing on semantic content via **Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)**. It was originally developed by Landauer, Foltz & Laham, and is used in Pearson’s products (such as the *WriteToLearn* platform and the PTE Academic exam).

6

IEA essentially compares the *meaning of an essay* to that of high-scoring essays or ideal responses by analyzing vector representations of the text. Using LSA, it can measure how well the essay covers the important concepts of the prompt by computing semantic similarity between the essay and reference texts or a domain knowledge space . This means IEA excels at assessing **content accuracy and relevance** – e.g., for a biology essay, it checks if key concepts are mentioned and in proper context by measuring semantic overlap with textbook material. Unlike e-rater and PEG, IEA is less focused on surface form (grammar or exact structure); it assumes that if the content is conceptually on-point, minor language errors can be overlooked. In practice, Pearson likely combines IEA’s content score with separate checks for grammar/mechanics. The literature notes that*“unlike PEG and e-rater, which attend to style and mechanics, IEA is an LSA-based tool designed to evaluate the conceptual content of essays.”* . IEA’s approach has proven powerful for certain use cases (like short-answer scoring and feedback for content-based writing). It is also used in **automated feedback**, giving students hints about missing points (since LSA can identify which relevant ideas from the prompt text or study materials were not covered in the essay). A limitation is that IEA requires a sizeable training set of essays scored by humans and/or a domain corpus to build the semantic space. It also may not directly gauge coherence or organization; an essay could be topically relevant (high semantic score) but poorly organized – IEA alone might miss that, which is why integrated systems consider multiple feature types.

- **Other AES Systems:** Several other commercial and research AES engines exist. **Vantage Learning’s IntelliMetric** is another long-running system, using an ensemble of NLP and AI techniques to mimic human scoring. It reportedly uses over 400 features, covering grammar, diction, organization, and ideas. IntelliMetric has been used in GMAT’s AWA scoring. Another system, **Bayesian Essay Test Scoring sYstem (BETSY)**, uses statistical classification (naive Bayes) on bag-of-words features – a simpler approach but surprisingly effective for certain tasks. These systems are generally proprietary, but their design principles echo those of e-rater and IEA: incorporate a broad range of linguistic and content features aligned to rubric criteria, and train on large datasets of human-scored essays.
- **Open-Source and Research Models:** In recent years, NLP research has produced many **open-** **source AES models**, often based on machine learning and deep learning. For example, Zhang and Litman (2016) applied neural networks to AES; and more recently **transformer-based models** (using BERT or GPT) have achieved strong results in essay scoring tasks . Some research has even fine- tuned large language models (LLMs) on scoring tasks, yielding human-level agreement on certain prompts . However, purely neural models can be black boxes – they predict a score but it’s hard to disentangle which features (coherence? vocabulary? argument quality?) led to that score. Hence, hybrid approaches are popular: using interpretable features alongside neural predictions for transparency. There are also toolkits like **LightSide** or **EssayScoring** that allow training custom AES models on a given dataset. One notable open dataset and competition was the **AEQ** (Automated Essay Quality) task, where models were trained to predict scores and sometimes also provide feedback. The **state-of-the-art (2020s)** in AES often combines text embeddings (to capture semantic content and writing style) with handcrafted features (to ensure coverage of traits like grammar errors or essay length). Such models can reach *QWK (quadratic weighted kappa)* agreements with human scores in the 0.70–0.85 range, which is on par with human raters in many contexts . Importantly, research emphasizes maintaining fairness (not scoring easier on certain demographics) and validity (truly measuring writing skill, not just correlating with essay length). **Coherence in AES Models:** Many AES systems include features for coherence or organization. For instance, e-rater, as noted, has discourse marker counts (assuming proper use of logical connectors improves coherence) . Some research systems use *entity grid scores* or *LSA-based coherence* as input features. There’s also work on scoring specific discourse elements – e.g. Persing & Ng (2015) built a model to score the *organization* of an essay separately from content. Likewise, **Somasundaran et al. (2014)** developed features targeting coherence structure (they used measures like sentence transition probabilities and synonym overlap, inspired by lexical cohesion) to predict scores on a coherence rubric . These studies showed that incorporating coherence-specific features can modestly improve overall scoring accuracy, especially on prompts or writing tasks where organization is a key differentiator. Another interesting line is using **argument structure** in AES: recent models (Stab & Gurevych, 2017; Wachsmuth et al., 2016) parse persuasive essays into argument components and evaluate the coherence of the argumentation (does the conclusion follow, are there gaps in reasoning). This overlaps with Toulmin’s model – an essay that presents claims with no evidence might be flagged by an argument-mining AES system and thus score lower on“argument coherence.” In sum, **modern AES models are increasingly multi-faceted**, not just counting words or errors but evaluating how well an essay is structured and whether it presents ideas in a connected, convincing manner.
