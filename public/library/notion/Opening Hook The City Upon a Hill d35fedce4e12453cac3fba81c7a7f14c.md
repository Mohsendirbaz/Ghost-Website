John Winthrop's 1630 sermon aboard the Arbella is perhaps the most frequently quoted text in American political discourse. "We shall be as a city upon a hill," he proclaimed, "the eyes of all people are upon us." Ronald Reagan invoked it. Barack Obama referenced it. It appears in high school civics textbooks as evidence of America's founding vision: a nation chosen by providence to serve as moral exemplar.

But Winthrop's sermon was not a declaration of triumphant destiny. It was a warning. The full context reveals anxiety, not confidence: "If we deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a by-word through the world." The city on a hill metaphor was conditional—obey God's law, or become an object lesson in failure.

More importantly, Winthrop delivered this sermon to a group of religious extremists fleeing expulsion, not seeking opportunity. The Massachusetts Bay Colony was founded by Puritans deemed too radical for England, where even Protestant reformers found their demands for biblical absolutism excessive. These were not seekers of religious freedom in any modern sense. They sought the freedom to establish their own theocracy, which they promptly did, banishing or executing those who disagreed.

Yet this is not how Americans remember it. The conditional warning has been transformed into unconditional promise. The religious extremists have become freedom fighters. The expulsion has been reframed as exodus. American exceptionalism—the belief that the United States is uniquely virtuous, uniquely destined, uniquely different from other nations—rests on this sanitized foundation.
